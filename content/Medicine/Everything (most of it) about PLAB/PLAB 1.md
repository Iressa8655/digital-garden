# My PLAB 1 Story

So. You're thinking about PLAB 1. First of all, condolences. Secondly, pour yourself a cup of tea, because I'm going to walk you through how this actually went, which is possibly more useful than the airbrushed accounts you'll find elsewhere.

## How I got here (the abbreviated version)

I started properly preparing for PLAB 1 in late 2024. Before that, I'd been simultaneously studying for USMLE and my Taiwan licensing exam, because clearly one licensing exam per country wasn't enough of a challenge. The upside was that the content overlapped reasonably well. The downside was that I was always preparing for a thing while also preparing for another thing, which is as fun as it sounds.

I sat my Taiwan exam in January 2025 and then pivoted hard to PLAB 1. That gave me about one to two months of focused prep. Not ideal. Not catastrophic. But there's a reason I'm mentioning it.

Also, quick warning. You will hear people online say PLAB 1 is "easier" than USMLE. These people are, to put it gently, mistaken. It isn't easier. It's different. And if you stroll in with your USMLE brain and no course correction, you will find yourself ambushed by a hundred and eighty questions that all seem to be asking slightly the wrong thing.

## Why your USMLE brain will betray you

USMLE is obsessed with mechanisms. It wants you to understand molecular choreography. Which receptor, which pathway, which downstream cascade. PLAB 1 does not care. PLAB 1 wants to know what you would actually do if a patient was in front of you at 3am on a Tuesday in Doncaster.

A few concrete examples:

**Brand names.** USMLE politely uses generic names. PLAB 1 will occasionally throw you a brand name and expect you to know it, like a trivia round you didn't sign up for. The first time this happened in practice, I genuinely thought it was a typo.

**Best answer, not correct answer.** You will stare at five options and all five will be perfectly reasonable things to do. Your job is to pick the most appropriate one. This sounds fine until you realise "best" is doing an enormous amount of work in that sentence, and "best" according to a junior doctor in the NHS, not according to your gut.

**Antibiotic mechanisms? Lovely. Not asked.** USMLE wants you to know that beta-lactam resistance happens because of altered penicillin-binding proteins. PLAB 1 would simply like to know what antibiotic you'd give the patient with the UTI. Please pick one. Thank you.

**Steroid potency.** Don't just know which steroids exist. Know which one to give a toddler with eczema on the face (answer: not clobetasol, unless you would also like to be struck off). PLAB 1 is very particular about this.

And finally, the one that defeated me most regularly: **referrals**. The UK has this thing called the NHS, and the NHS has this thing called primary care gatekeeping, and in Taiwan we don't really do any of that. So I spent a genuinely confusing amount of time trying to work out whether the scenario in front of me was a "manage in GP", a "refer to secondary care", or a "blue-light ambulance, immediately". That muscle does not develop by osmosis. You have to learn it, deliberately, like an adult.

## What I actually used

Right. Real talk on resources, because I had about a month of real prep time and I wasn't going to sit down and read a textbook cover to cover like a disciplined medical student from a Pixar film.

**NICE guidelines** are your bible. Most questions map straight back to NICE. The website is, against all expectations for a government body, quite well organised. If you had to pick one resource and delete the rest, make it this one.

**Oxford Handbook of Clinical Medicine** is a good reference. I didn't read it cover to cover. No one reads it cover to cover. It is a book you open when you realise you don't know something, and then close quickly before you have to confront how much else you don't know.

**Plabable** is where I got my qbank. Doing questions is, by a country mile, the most useful thing you can do for PLAB 1. Reading alone will not save you. You must bash through questions until the style of the exam has burrowed itself into your subconscious.

And then, the headline act: **the Plabable Revision Course**. Four days. Fully online and live, 10am to 5:30pm UK time. £130 for the entire thing. Aligned with the latest MLA content map, which matters, because the exam content moves around and nobody sends you a postcard about it. Taught by Dr Adam Jakes (obs and gynae, and, against all expectation for anyone teaching gynae at hour six of day three, genuinely funny) and Dr Wei Zian Szetho, known to all as "Sze". Both excellent. There's a dedicated WhatsApp group where you can bother them with questions, and a big end-of-course mock that is worth the ticket price on its own.

I will say this without embellishment: without this course, I would not have passed PLAB 1. Book it. Book it now. Book it before you finish reading this sentence.

## The exam itself

PLAB 1 is 180 single best answer questions in three hours. That's 60 seconds per question. You should aim for 45. The leftover 15 seconds is for the questions where you have no idea what's going on and need a moment to make peace with it.

Time management is the single most underrated skill for this exam. Drill it from day one of your qbank. Train yourself to commit to an answer and move on, even when you're about 63% sure. Doubt is a luxury that 180 questions in 180 minutes does not permit.

## Exam day

I tried to book a London slot. There were no London slots. There are, apparently, never any London slots. So I went to Oxford, which turned out to be fine, because the test centre is about ninety seconds' walk from the station. I booked an Airbnb nearby. Walked over in the morning. If you're planning the trip and want specifics, drop me a message.

The venue itself is a large gymnasium with individual desks laid out like the world's most stressful school sports day. You're allowed a small snack (I brought chocolate and an apple, because I am a sensible adult who understands blood sugar), plus pencils, an eraser, and a sharpener. That's the list. No phones. No notes. No emotional support animals.

It's a paper exam, and you answer by filling in little black circles. So basically a Year 11 SATs paper, but with slightly more riding on it and considerably worse handwriting.

One genuinely important tip: fill in your answers on the sheet as you go. Do not leave them to transfer at the end. You will run out of time. You will have a small, quiet breakdown. You will haunt me in your dreams for not warning you, except I did warn you, so that's on you.

The staff, for the record, are lovely. Worryingly cheerful, actually. Like they didn't get the memo about us all being nervous wrecks.

## What it actually felt like

I want to be honest about this bit, because most online PLAB 1 content appears to have been written by someone who was quietly top of the class and has no memory of human suffering. That was not me.

I spent the exam counting the questions I wasn't sure about. By about question 90, I had decided I'd failed. I rang my mum afterwards to tell her I had failed. (My mum, for the record, was sceptical. Mums usually are.)

There's a WhatsApp group where people post recalled questions after the exam, and we all compare notes and gently unravel together. Even the people in there who had properly prepared were uncertain about the same questions I was. The ones who finished early and remembered everything perfectly and felt great: statistical outliers. Don't compare yourself to them. They are not real.

My actual score: 137. Pass mark: 117. Average: 124. So, comfortably above average, despite feeling, at the time, like I was scraping the bottom of the barrel of human cognition.

The wait for results was about five and a half weeks. I sat the exam on 20 February. I got my result on 28 March. The intervening period was, in technical terms, dreadful.

## The one thing I wish I'd known earlier

The UK is not looking for an outstanding doctor. It's looking for a safe doctor. That is the entire bar. That is the whole thing.

Once I properly internalised this, roughly two weeks into prep and about six weeks too late, my anxiety dropped considerably. You don't need to know the ultra-rare zebra diagnosis. You need to know when to treat, when to refer, and when to shout for help. If you carry that frame into the exam, the questions start making a lot more sense.

## Some context, in case you're wondering how

In case you're wondering how I fit all this in: I didn't, really. I was also doing a Master's at UCL. In London. Having moved from Taiwan. I also, in my spare time, had to buy milk occasionally, which felt like a lot.

The week before my exam I was locked into an intensive week-long course as part of my Master's programme. So my final-stretch revision was, shall we say, compromised. The Plabable revision course truly did carry me. That is not an exaggeration. I would not be writing this otherwise. I would be sobbing quietly somewhere instead.

## If I were telling a friend over drinks

- Don't assume USMLE prep will carry you. The science overlaps. The style doesn't.
- Build your revision around NICE. Treat it like scripture.
- Use Plabable. Do the questions. Do more questions. Then do a few more.
- Book the revision course. I realise I keep saying this. That is because it is the single most important piece of advice in this post.
- Drill time management. 45 seconds. No lingering.
- Learn UK referral logic deliberately, especially if you come from a non-GP-gatekeeping system. It will not come to you by magic.

And, above all: safe doctor, not perfect doctor.

You can do this, even when life is actively trying to prevent it. I did, and my prep was held together with string, caffeine, and Dr Sze's teaching slides. The UK system, for all its quirks, is welcoming once you're through the gate. Drop me a message if you want to talk any of it through. I'm happy to help, and I will, if nothing else, confirm that yes, it did feel that bad at the time, and yes, you will still be fine.
