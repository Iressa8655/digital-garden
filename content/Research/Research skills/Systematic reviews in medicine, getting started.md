# Systematic reviews in medicine, getting started

My notes from the Bodleian Libraries' iSkills workshop *Systematic reviews and scoping reviews in medicine, Part 1, getting started* (Oxford, 3 November 2026), led by Neal Thurley and Nia Roberts. The process is theirs. The summary and the opinions are mine. If you are at Oxford, the [iSkills workshops](https://www.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/ask/workshops#/) are free and worth booking.

The session was deliberately theoretical. It walked through the search process that sits underneath any systematic, scoping or rapid review, whichever of the three you end up doing.

---

## 1. Start with the question

Everything downstream depends on it. A vague question gives you a search you cannot finish and a screening stage you cannot defend.

## 2. Search before you search

Run preliminary searches before you commit. The point is to find out whether someone has already done your review, and if so, when and how well. An old or weak review is a reason to do yours. A recent, good one is a reason to change your question.

## 3. Register a protocol

A protocol sets out in advance exactly what you will do. In medicine the usual home is [PROSPERO](https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/), though there are others. Some journals will not publish a systematic review without a registered protocol, so this is not optional paperwork.

## 4. Search widely, then chase citations

The core databases are [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), [Web of Science](https://www.webofscience.com/) and [Scopus](https://www.scopus.com/). For related-article and citation searching, [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com/) casts the widest net, because it reaches beyond journal articles into the broader academic literature.

## 5. Screen in duplicate

The gold standard is that **two people independently assess every record**, first at title and abstract, then at full text. Data extraction and quality assessment are usually done in duplicate too.

At that level a spreadsheet stops being enough, and review management software earns its keep.

| Tool | Who it suits | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| [Covidence](https://www.covidence.org/) | Oxford staff and students via single sign-on ([how to log in](https://support.covidence.org/doc/university-of-oxford)); external collaborators can be invited | free to Oxford users |
| [Rayyan](https://www.rayyan.ai/) | anyone, including NHS colleagues | free tier with limited features |
| [Abstrackr](http://abstrackr.cebm.brown.edu/), [CADIMA](https://www.cadima.info/), [Colandr](https://www.colandrapp.com/) | anyone | free |

The library does not endorse any one of them. If you cannot use Covidence, compare the others for your own review. A [2023 study in *Systematic Reviews*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10105467/) is a useful starting point on how well their screening features perform.

## 6. Keep your numbers as you go

Every record retrieved, every duplicate removed, every exclusion and its reason. These numbers become your [PRISMA flow diagram](https://www.prisma-statement.org/prisma-2020-flow-diagram), and they are miserable to reconstruct afterwards.

You also need a narrative account of the search (where, when, which terms, what grey literature), summarised in the methods. The exact search strings go in an appendix or the online supplement.

---

## Where to read next

When I asked a health care librarian afterwards where to go for the detail, the answer was two places.

1. **Scoping reviews.** The [JBI scoping review resources](https://jbi.global/scoping-review-network/resources), which include protocol templates.
2. **Systematic reviews.** The [Cochrane Handbook](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current), the standard reference.

The other advice was practical. Before you ask anyone for help, draft your protocol and run a few simple searches in PubMed or Google Scholar, so you know roughly how much literature is out there.

## Where AI fits, for now

This was the part I came for.

The honest position from the session is that AI can help with **specific tasks, under human supervision**. There are plenty of evaluations of different tools on different tasks, with mixed results. What nobody can yet claim is that one tool runs the whole review on its own, and probably not even with a human checking the output.

The guidance to read is [RAISE](https://www.cochrane.org/events/recommendations-and-guidance-responsible-ai-evidence-synthesis) (Responsible AI use in evidence SynthEsis), which sets out where AI support has been shown to help and how to check that it is doing what you think it is doing.

As a doctor moving into AI research, I find this the right kind of caution. The interesting question is not whether AI can do a systematic review. It is which step you can safely hand over, and what check you put in place before you trust it.
