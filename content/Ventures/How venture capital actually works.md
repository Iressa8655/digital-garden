# How venture capital actually works

I spend part of my week reading pitch decks as a scout for an early-stage AI fund. The first thing that surprised me is how little of the judgement is about whether a company is *good*. Plenty of good companies are a bad fit for venture capital, and understanding why requires understanding the arithmetic the fund itself is trapped inside.

This is that arithmetic, written out plainly, followed by the three things I actually look for when a deck lands in my inbox.

> [!info] What this is not
> Everything below is general venture economics, the kind of thing you would find in any fund's public writing or an industry returns report. Nothing here is specific to my own fund's strategy, portfolio or internal process.

---

## 1. The fund is a promise with a deadline

A venture fund is not a person with opinions. It is a **closed-ended vehicle** with a fixed pot of money, a fixed lifetime of roughly ten years, and a set of investors called **LPs** (limited partners) who have been promised something specific.

The promise has to clear a fairly obvious bar. If an LP had simply put the money into a global index fund, they would have done reasonably well over a decade, with the considerable luxury of being able to sell on any given Tuesday. Venture offers the opposite of that luxury. The money is locked up for years and a large fraction of it will be lost.

So venture funds have to promise **materially more than the index**, typically in the region of three to five times the money in, and they have to do it while being completely illiquid. That gap between what an index does and what the fund promised is the pressure behind every decision that follows.

## 2. Not all of the fund gets invested

Two things come out before a single pound reaches a founder.

**Running the fund costs money.** A regulated fund has auditors, administrators, legal counsel, compliance, and staff. Over a fund's life this is a meaningful slice of the total.

**Reserves get held back.** When a portfolio company raises its next round, the fund usually wants to put more in to defend its position, or to bridge a company that is close but not quite there. That money has to be set aside at the start.

What is left, the capital that actually goes into new companies, is substantially smaller than the headline fund size. Every one of those cheques now has to work harder than the headline number suggests.

## 3. Dilution is relentless

Say a fund writes an early cheque and ends up owning five per cent of a company. That five per cent does not stay five per cent.

Every subsequent round issues new shares. So does every expansion of the employee option pool. A company that raises a seed, a Series A, a Series B and grows its team will dilute its early investors at each step, and the effect compounds. Early ownership of five per cent routinely lands somewhere between one and two per cent by the time of an exit.

This is not a pathology, it is how the instrument works. But it means **the exit has to be much larger than intuition suggests** for the early cheque to matter.

## 4. Why a very good outcome can still be a bad outcome

Here is the part that took me longest to accept.

Imagine a company sells for $250 million. For the founders this is life-changing money. For most people, on most definitions, this is an unambiguous success.

Now run it through the arithmetic. A fund that started with five per cent and diluted down to roughly two per cent takes home about $6 million. Against a $1 million cheque that is a six-fold return, which sounds excellent, and in isolation it is.

But the fund promised its LPs several times the entire fund. A $6 million return against a target measured in the hundreds of millions is a rounding error. The fund has just used up one of its limited number of shots on goal and moved barely a few per cent toward its obligation.

> [!tip] The uncomfortable conclusion
> A great outcome for the founders can be a **missed shot** for the fund. This is not cynicism, it is structural, and it explains most of the refusals that early-stage investors give to obviously competent teams.

## 5. So the bar is, could this one company return the whole fund?

Working backwards, each investment has to carry a **credible path to returning the entire fund on its own**. Not a likely path. A credible one.

Push the dilution maths through and the exit size required to do that is enormous, typically in the billions rather than the hundreds of millions. There are not many companies in any given year that could plausibly get there.

That is why early-stage investors are so unreasonably picky about market size, and why "this is a real business with real customers" is not, by itself, an argument that wins. If there is no visible route to a very large outcome, the arithmetic simply does not close, however much everyone likes the team.

## 6. How funds get measured, and how few succeed

Two numbers matter.

**DPI**, distributed to paid-in, is cash actually returned to LPs divided by cash they put in. It is the only number that cannot be argued with.

**MOIC** or TVPI is the current value of the portfolio over capital invested. It includes companies that have not sold yet, so it is an opinion dressed as a number until the exits land.

The public data on how funds actually perform is sobering, and worth sitting with. Analyses of long-run European and US fund vintages consistently show that a large share of funds fail to beat a public index, that only a small top slice return several times the money net of fees, and that it takes the better part of a decade before LPs see meaningful cash back at all ([British Business Bank research and publications](https://www.british-business-bank.co.uk/research-and-publications)).

And within the funds that do succeed, the wins are not spread evenly. Top funds do not get there by having fewer failures. They get there by having **bigger hits**. A large fraction of deals in even an excellent fund go to zero, and the overwhelming majority of the return comes from a very small number of positions.

> [!abstract] The whole model in one line
> Venture is not a business of avoiding losses. It is a business of **being in the one or two companies that make everything else irrelevant**.

## 7. What I actually look for

Given all of the above, a scout's job is narrow, find the small number of companies with a plausible route to being that outlier. Three questions do most of the work.

### 7.1. Is this an outlier team?

Outlier does not mean impressive. It means genuinely exceptional, in the specific thing this company requires.

The useful move is to ask what this company is actually hard at. A company whose core risk is a scientific one needs world-class researchers. One whose core risk is distribution needs people with real access to that industry. One whose core risk is infrastructure needs people who have built and run systems of that kind at scale. Being excellent on the wrong axis does not count.

And the bar is genuinely high. There is a meaningful gap between good, great, and outlier, and only the last one is the thing being looked for.

> [!warning] Verify, do not quote
> The single most common failure when writing a company up is repeating what the founder says about themselves. A pitch is a sales document. If someone is described as a leading researcher, their publication record is public. If someone is described as a formidable engineer, their code usually is too. Check it, then write down what you checked and what you found.

A related trap is the brilliant technical founder who cannot build a company. Someone has to hire, sell, raise, partner and pitch. If the technical founder is not that person, there needs to be someone beside them who is.

### 7.2. Is there a credible path to a very large business?

The practical proxy is whether there is a plausible route to $100 million in annual recurring revenue, because that is roughly the scale that supports a billion-dollar-plus valuation.

That is a question about the market in three parts. **Size**, because almost no company takes more than a modest share of its market, so the market has to be very large, or credibly about to become very large. **Shape**, meaning who the players are and whether this is a winner-takes-most structure or one the incumbents quietly absorb. **State**, meaning whether the opportunity is still open or the moment has already been captured.

Then do the arithmetic from the bottom up rather than the top down. If they charge a certain amount, how many customers does $100 million require, and does that number of customers exist? Is that spending already in someone's budget, or does it have to be created? And because it is *recurring* revenue, is there a reason the margin does not erode the moment a competitor appears?

> [!note] Focus on the upside, not the risk
> This is the counter-intuitive bit. Early-stage companies are all fragile, and they will break in ways nobody predicted. Cataloguing the risks is easy and not very informative. The harder and more useful exercise is, **assuming they execute well, how big can this actually get?** That number is what decides whether the investment can work at all.

### 7.3. Is the moat real?

The defining risk in AI right now is **commoditisation**. Every time a new frontier model ships, a large number of startups whose entire product was a layer on top of the previous one quietly stop making sense.

So the question is not whether the company is useful in the next twelve months. It is whether it still has a right to exist in three to five years, after several more model generations have arrived. Usually that comes down to some combination of proprietary data that accumulates with use, and genuine domain expertise that is hard to hire, embedded somewhere a general-purpose model cannot easily reach.

Thin wrappers, prompt layers and orchestration harnesses tend to get absorbed into the models themselves. A moat built out of data that gets deeper the longer the company operates tends not to.

---

## 8. The one-page version

1. The fund owes its investors several times the money, on a ten-year clock, with no liquidity.
2. Costs and reserves mean less gets invested than the headline size suggests.
3. Dilution grinds early ownership down to a fraction of where it started.
4. So a $250 million exit, wonderful for founders, can be immaterial to the fund.
5. Therefore every investment needs a credible path to returning the fund alone.
6. And in practice, one or two companies produce nearly all of any fund's return.
7. Which leaves three questions, outlier team, credible path to very large, and a moat that survives the next few model generations.

None of this makes venture a good way to finance most companies. It makes it a very specific instrument for a very specific shape of company, and most of the skill is in telling the difference quickly.
